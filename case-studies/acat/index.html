Case Study: Designing for "A" User

One extraordinary user, and a design discipline built around him. Our chairman of the board and partner Pete Denman led the user interface design of ACAT, the Assistive Context Aware Toolkit: the system Intel built to keep Stephen Hawking talking as his abilities changed. It doubled his typing speed, was released to the world as free open source software, and Pete kept leading its design for the wider AAC community for a decade after.

The challenge

One user. One muscle. The whole world listening.

By 2011, ALS had taken everything but a single reliable movement: a twitch of Stephen Hawking's cheek, read by an infrared sensor on his glasses. With it he ran a communication system whose core interaction dated to the 1980s, character by scanned character, and his rate had slowed to roughly a word a minute. Lectures, email, books, and every spoken sentence went through that one switch.

He asked Intel for help, and Intel asked Pete to lead the interface design. The brief was a paradox: make the system radically faster for a user with one input, without taking away an interface that was, after three decades, part of how he thought. The voice was not negotiable. The habits were not negotiable. The output had to change anyway.

Approach and build

The approach

Study the user, not the technology

This was participatory design: Stephen was not a test subject, he was a codesigner. The team spent years in the work, visiting him at his Cambridge office and home, watching how he actually used the system rather than how anyone assumed he did. Pete's first insight came before the first prototype: watching video of Stephen straining his eyes to the left and right, he realized the world's most famous physicist had no way to see behind his own chair, and mounted a small mirror on the monitor. The biggest design decision of all was restraint. Stephen had lived inside his interface for decades, so the team deliberately kept what he saw familiar, holding him within the affordances he knew, while the technology underneath changed completely • observe closely • preserve what the user trusts • let his reaction, not the roadmap, decide what ships.

The build

A toolkit, not a gadget

ACAT wrapped the whole computer around that one switch: keyboard simulation with next word prediction, so fewer than a fifth of characters had to be typed, plus context aware menus that automated multistep chores into single selections • talking • lectures • email and files • browsing. At its public debut, typing speed had doubled and common tasks ran about ten times faster. The input stayed exactly where Stephen wanted it: his cheek, his rhythm, his voice.

The use case scenario

One voice, kept. A composite user, from diagnosis onward.

ACAT was designed for one user and released for many. "Ana" is a composite built from the populations the research documented: motor neuron disease, stroke, brain injury, and the family members who set systems up. Her arc is the argument for a toolkit that adapts as abilities change.

  • Month 0

    The diagnosis

    Ana is 58 when ALS is confirmed, the bulbar kind that takes speech first. The clinic's timeline is blunt: most people like her lose functional speech within about three years. Her voice is her classroom, her phone calls with her mother, her jokes. The question is not whether she will need another way to talk. It is whether that way will still sound like her.

  • Month 2

    Setup, by someone who loves her

    Her daughter downloads ACAT for free on a Saturday. The onboarding is built for exactly this person: a motivated relative, not a technician, walking through welcome, input selection, and testing in one sitting. The research behind it watched real families do this and fixed what confused them, because a system a family cannot set up is a system the user never gets.

  • Month 3

    Finding her switch

    While Ana still has strong hands, a simple button works. They test the alternatives anyway: the webcam that reads an eyebrow raise, the proximity sensor that reads a cheek, the same family of triggers Stephen used. Any of them can drive the whole system, so the switch can follow her body instead of her body chasing the switch.

  • Month 6

    The first fluent sentence

    Scanning highlights groups of letters; her switch picks one; prediction does the rest. She types a handful of characters and the system offers the sentence she was reaching for. The synthesized voice speaks it to the kitchen. It is not her old voice. It is unmistakably her sentence, at a speed a conversation can survive.

  • Month 10

    Keeping up at the dinner table

    Group conversation is the hardest thing an AAC user does: by the time a reply is typed, the topic has moved. The contextual response work in ACAT's redesign listens to the conversation and drafts likely responses in context, so Ana lands a reply, and a joke, while it still counts. Five documented design iterations went into getting that right.

  • Month 14

    The phrase bank

    The sentences she says every day live one selection deep: greetings, care instructions, the exact words for how her body feels, and the sharp remarks she refuses to give up. Categories keep them findable. A voice is not only new sentences. It is the old ones, ready when she wants them.

  • Year 3

    When the body changes again

    The button goes first, then the reliable cheek movement. ACAT's answer is the reason it is a toolkit: for users approaching a locked in state, it supports noninvasive EEG brain computer interfaces as simply another switch. The interface she has spent three years mastering stays. Only the trigger changes.

  • Always

    What no feature replaces

    Every design decision serves the same rule the Cambridge visits taught: the user's words, the user's timing, the user's identity. ACAT never speaks for Ana. It clears the path between what she means and what the room hears, and her family gets her, not a transcript of her.

Effort down, expression up

Every layer exists to spend the user's scarcest resource, effort, on meaning instead of mechanics: fewer than a fifth of characters typed, chores collapsed into single selections.

The user decides what it says. Always.

  • their words, never autopilot
  • their timing, never a prompt rushed
  • their voice, kept for decades

Drawn from the shipped system and its documentation. The input list grew over a decade, from Stephen's infrared cheek sensor to camera gestures and EEG, without the user ever having to relearn the interface on the other side.

The outcome

Built for one. Released to everyone.

The ACAT mark, cut out of the background.

The system debuted publicly in London in December 2014, with Stephen Hawking on stage describing what it gave back to him. Intel put a number on the ambition: the same platform could be adapted for millions of people living with motor neuron disease and quadriplegia. Open source was a cause Pete and Stephen championed together, and in August 2015 Intel released ACAT as free software, so that developers, researchers, and families could take the system built for the most famous disabled man on earth and fit it to anyone.

Stephen Hawking died in March 2018. Most projects built for one person die with them. This one did not, because the open source decision he and Pete had pushed for had already given it a life beyond any single user. Freed from the constraint of one man's decades of habit, the team began expanding the software in the years after his passing: brain computer interface input for people approaching a locked in state, sentence prediction, and a complete overhaul of the interface, all while keeping the technologies created for Stephen in place underneath.

Pete led that design work through 2025. The redesign refit ACAT for the wider AAC community: onboarding a family member can complete without a technician, camera and BCI inputs alongside switches, and the contextual response system that helps users keep pace with live conversation. Formal usability research in 2023 validated the flows with real participants, and surfaced something nobody had planned for: people with aphasia finding a use for a tool built for motor neuron disease. Research done with the community keeps returning more than it was asked for.

Design for one user, done honestly, turns out to be design for many. The discipline this project forged, begin with one person's lived reality, cocreate, validate, then hand the results to the world, is the same arc our Connected Wheelchair case study follows, and the method Access Insights now runs deliberately.

For Pete, the project was never abstract. A quadriplegic designer who navigates the world from a power chair, he was chosen to lead this work in part because he understood, bodily, what it means to depend on technology that must not fail you. He has called being dyslexic one of the most powerful things he has going for him: a mind that reads systems sideways found the mirror on the monitor, and everything that followed.

What this case proves

Lived experience is a research method.

ACAT followed the arc Access Insights now exists to run deliberately: begin with the community's lived experience, involve the people affected as cocreators, turn insight into evidence and working software, validate with real users, and give the results a pathway into the world. Here the pathway was open source, and the validation continued for a decade.

It produced a communication system trusted with one of the most valuable voices of our time, then handed that system to everyone who needs one. That happened because disabled expertise held design authority from the first visit to the last release. We are building the infrastructure to make that the rule, not the exception.
